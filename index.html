Geometric Neuron / Experiment 1

Two deerskins meet.

What if a neuron listens to another through its own moving surface? The sender broadcasts the 16 samples of its checkerboard. The receiver's present checkerboard becomes its receptor mosaic. Their overlap determines how much one affects the other.

The original 59-tick forgetting clock is unchanged. No training, no imported theta rhythm.

The first live panel below is the newest result, the setpoint wire. Then the constant on the setpoint port, and the original two-surface lab. Second round: why the colony goes silent, and a hold-and-release switch. Third round: a two-way chain taught by that switch alone. Fourth round: what the checkerboard history tells a listener that beat times do not. Fifth round: a receptor learned from those rows replays a taught chain at the taught spacing. Sixth round: a free colony keeps whichever taught order it is cued into. Seventh round: that kept state is a root, and two sequences can share a unit. Eighth round: a unit makes another beat through the setpoint port, with no trigger. Tenth round: a silent unit is an exact relay and keeps nothing.

Live · rounds eight and ten · 11 November 2026

Four units wired through the setpoint port

Each unit's two beat wires (out_0, out_1) go to the next unit's setpoint_mod, the port the original node always had. When a unit beats, its neighbour's setpoint dips, and the neighbour beats through its own feedback loop 4 ticks later. Nothing overrides any output: no trigger, no refractory, no learning.

Unit 0 keeps the original constant (1.0) and beats by itself. Units 1 to 3 start with their Constant Signal at 1.6, where a unit has no beats of its own (ninth round), so every beat you see on them was passed along the wire.

Things to try

Excite puts a 2-tick pulse on that unit's setpoint port. A silent unit (constant 1.6) beat exactly 4 ticks later at 59 of 59 moments tried, once, and then went quiet again. A unit with its own clock (constant 1.0) did so at 48 of 59 phases. Hold is the 8-tick rhythm from the second round.

What changed

Original colony: Rᵢ = ownᵢ + Σⱼ wᵢⱼ tapsⱼ[6:10]. Sender decides what is available; the listener has no geometric receptive state.

This experiment: Rᵢ = ownᵢ + g Σⱼ wᵢⱼ K(Gᵢ,Gⱼ). Both surfaces participate in the interaction.

Here K is a mean-centered, normalized 16-sample inner product. The raw uncentered version is an explicit control; it is not equivalent.

What did the test find?

Across 59 receiver phases, stimulation changes the next beat in some phases and not others. The moving surface is a genuine state-dependent communication operator.

But the untrained six-unit colony is fragile. At uniformly coupled weight 0.05, moving-surface units average 3 silent units out of 6; at 0.10 all six are silent. The geometry does not magically solve stability or learning.

We also tested raw overlap, fixed template, shifted surface, original taps and no communication. The original taps often give stronger phase effects than our new operator. There is no measured task advantage.

Second round · Fable 5.2 · 10 November 2026

Why it goes silent, and what the same rhythm is good for

Every unit idles with a small ripple that repeats every 8 ticks, twice its loop delay. The unit is tuned to that ripple. A square wave of amplitude 0.1 at that period stops one unit beating completely. A constant input needs about 0.5 to do the same.

Idle neighbours send each other exactly this ripple through the overlap, which is how a colony talks itself into silence. The shifted-surface control shows it cleanly: its average heard input is about zero, yet all six units go silent at weight 0.05, and removing the 8-tick band from what is heard brings all six back.

No repair fixes the moving-surface colony at every strength. Three were tried: removing the 8-tick band, subtracting the heard signal's own 50-tick average, and keeping the heard signal out of the variance memory. The last is the best single one (0 silent of 6 at weight 0.05, 0.9 at 0.10). All three together give 0 silent at 0.10, but at about a third of the normal beat rate. At 0.20 nothing keeps all six alive. All three are engineered additions.

Hold and release

Press Hold A above: A stops within one cycle while B carries on. Press it again to release. From 48 random starting phases, a unit held with amplitude 0.2 beat exactly 53 ticks after release, every time. The rhythm erases the unit's phase and release starts its clock.

Six unconnected units released 8 ticks apart beat in the order of release in 24 of 24 trials (0 of 24 without the hold). So a pattern of timing can be written into a colony from outside, with no weights changed.

Third round · Fable 5.2 · 10 November 2026

A chain taught by hold and release

Nothing is forced during teaching. Units 0 to 3 are held silent by the rhythm, then released a few ticks apart, alternating the order 0→1→2→3 and 3→2→1→0. Every beat is the loop's own. A count of which beat came just before which sets the weights, which are then frozen. Then one end is cued.

Fourth round · Fable 5.2 · 10 November 2026

What the checkerboard history tells a listener

A unit's rows form a sequence in time. One unit lives a disturbed life (random holds and pulses), and a reader has to call each beat before it happens. Readers are plain lookup tables, so the comparison is about what each signal contains.

Beat times are a clock: perfect until the unit is disturbed. The last four rows warn of over 90% of disturbed beats, four ticks early, which is the loop delay. For that warning the order of the rows does not matter. Order matters only for the long view: 32 rows in order call every regular beat 8 ticks ahead, and none when shuffled.

The reader here is a table outside the loop, not a receptor inside a colony. The hand-written trigger from the earlier rounds ignores this warning: it waits for the beat and then overrides the listener's output. Numbers: results/round4.json.

Fifth round · Fable 5.2 · 10 November 2026

A receptor learned from a neighbour's rows

The units are unconnected while they are taught, by hold and release, in both orders. Each unit learns one thing per neighbour: a table over that neighbour's last one to three checkerboard rows. At its own beat it looks back 9 ticks at what the neighbour that beat just before it was showing. There are no connection weights. At test the four units are held silent and one end is cued.

Every replay with the right order also had every hop within one tick of the taught lag. A cue never produced the opposite order, and with no cue a taught colony stayed silent. The beat tally of the third round replays at 5 ticks per hop whatever it was taught; this receptor replays the spacing it was taught.

A receptor that sees only the current row scores 0 of 12 at every gap. Throwing away the order of the rows in its window changes nothing. Random-order teaching and no teaching both score 0.

It works only for hops of 6 to 8 ticks. The trigger is hand-written: it plays the unit's own recorded beat by overriding its output, and is on only at test. Also hand-set: a 30-tick refractory, nearest-predecessor credit (Sol's rule), a 30% threshold, and the hold at test. Each test is deterministic once the colony is held, so these are 12 colonies, not 96 trials. Numbers: results/round5.json.

Sixth round · Fable 5.2 · 11 November 2026

A free colony keeps the order it is cued into

Two checks on the fifth round. First, the lookup table is not needed: a plain linear filter over a neighbour's last three rows (48 numbers) replays the chain just as well, 11 or 12 colonies of 12 at hops of 6 to 8 ticks. A filter that sees only beat times scores 0 there, because the listener must decide before the neighbour's beat has happened. Beat times take over at a hop of 10.

Second, the test with nothing held. Every unit keeps its own 59-tick beat. The four units are erased (held by the rhythm, released together), one end is cued once, and then the colony is left alone.

With no receptors, or receptors taught on random orders, no trial ends in a taught order (0 of 72 each). One cue without an erase does not switch the order: it leaves it in about half the trials and breaks it in the rest.

So the colony has somewhere to hold "which order we are in": the beat pattern itself, chosen by one cue, kept with no weight changed.

The trigger, refractory, credit rule and threshold are hand-built, the filters are fitted offline, and the erase comes from outside. Nothing has been tested on a task. Numbers: results/round6.json.

Seventh round · Fable 5.2 · 11 November 2026 · checks proposed by Sol and Gemini

The working state is a root

With every beat logged, a written order either forms and repeats exactly or does not form: 1,200 of 1,200 cycles clean at a taught hop of 6; at hop 8, 23 of 24 written orders clean and one that never formed. The period is always 59, the leader's own clock. The leader's beats are its own; every follower's beat is triggered.

So the receptors hold a graph with travel times, the beat pattern holds one address in it, and each cycle a wave from that address gives every unit its distance. Hold the root silent and the next unit takes over as root (12 of 12). Hold the followers and they are still triggered every cycle.

The checkerboard is one workable lift, not a special one

All 16 numbers of a row come from one integer, the square size. A receptor over that integer, its square and its cube for the last three ticks matches the 48-number checkerboard receptor (12 of 12 both ways) once its threshold is set. A linear receptor on the integer scores 0. The checkerboard's only measured advantage is that it is less sensitive to the threshold.

The summed receptor that solves the fork uses the cube of the square size plus beat history; its checkerboard version mostly fails. In a free colony it keeps a cued order only at hop 6. The trigger, refractory, thresholds and the erase are still hand-built, and nothing has been tested on a task. Numbers: results/round7.json.

Tenth round · Fable 5.2 · 11 November 2026 · experiment proposed by Sol

A silent unit is an exact relay, and keeps nothing

Set a unit's constant to 1.45 or more and it has no beats of its own, from 12 of 12 random starts. Then give it the same 2-tick setpoint pulse that round eight used.

The silent unit is more excitable than the beating one, answers 4 ticks after the pulse, and answers a second pulse at every gap from 2 to 59 ticks. With unit A beating by itself and wired to silent B at weight −5.5 or −7, B beat once after every beat of A (51 of 51). When A was silenced, B had no beats in the next 1,500 ticks.

So Sol's three questions come out as: excitable, yes; recovery, immediate; persistence, none. At each constant the unit has one fate. The wire is a gate and a relay, not a memory. A chain of eight such units passed 50 of 50 beats to the far end at 4 ticks per hop.

The pulse only works for setpoint dips of about −8 to −12 on the port; shallower or deeper does nothing. The chain works in a band of weights (−5 to −7) with a hole at −5.5. Both directions still run away. Numbers: results/round10.json.

Scientific boundary: these are real-valued sampled images and Pearson correlation. Their overlap is reciprocal at one instant. It is not quantum interference, not learned attention, and not proof of task-dependent neural communication. The original closed-loop controller and sample table are copied unchanged for audit.
